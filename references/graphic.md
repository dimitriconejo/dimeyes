# His graphic hand: posters, posts, stories, print and templates

How Dimitri composes, corrects and judges a graphic piece. Read it whole, in a call of its own, before the first graphic piece of a session, after SKILL.md. It is complete for graphic pieces. It serves every graphic piece in any style: the piece's own DNA (SKILL.md, Respect the piece) decides the look, and this file decides the execution.

Values are px on a 1080-wide canvas: scale them for other sizes. Where a number comes from his tests or the pieces he signed, the line says so; the others are common craft defaults not yet checked with him. None is a quota: the render at the size of use decides. Of his comments on catalogue rounds, about a third are pure quality verdicts (*"really ugly"* (*"feisimo"*), *"it makes no sense"* (*"sin sentido"*), *"I want something professional"* (*"quiero algo profesional"*)), about a third spatial relationships and about a fifth the signature: §2, §3 and §9 answer them.

## 1. Before you touch it

- **Render it where it lives.** Social formats are 1080 wide and shown at about 390 pt on a phone: divide px by about 2.8 to know what the reader sees. Look at each format on its own at phone width, then at 100 %.
- **Use the real canvas, safe area and brand slot** of the product. The product's safe area wins over the platform's (§10). Read the brand placement from the template's source.
- **Brand it before you judge it.** A piece a customer will sign is judged with a sample mark in its slot, the same mark across every alternative; hide it once to check the piece still feels complete without it.
- **Signed references.** When `assets/calibration/` exists, view the signed piece closest to yours before your first verdict of the session, and put your render beside it.
- **Write the diagnosis before editing** (SKILL.md, method steps 1–4): first, second and third read; the grade; the DNA; the first break and every defect; the depth.

## 2. Composition

**One protagonist that clearly wins.** A title that spans the width, a quotation that fills the height, a photograph at full bleed, a hero figure. In the posts by other designers he would sign, the protagonist covered about 46 % of the canvas, against 34 % in the ones he rejected, where two masses of similar weight competed. Those were art and culture posts with little text: take the relationship, never a share to reach by shrinking what a piece carries. If two elements compete for the lead, make one lead or fuse them into one shape.

**Three levels you can see in two seconds.** Lead, support, and the practical data with the signature. At about 180 px wide (a thumbnail), the order 1 → 2 → 3 is obvious and nothing competes for first place. A competing heading becomes a small kicker or joins the title.

**Groups, not a chain of bands.** Unrelated blocks at similar medium gaps down the canvas read as *"little visual hierarchy, little composition"* (*"poca jerarquía visual, poca composición"*). Group by meaning, then either anchor the groups to different parts of the frame (the top, the foot, a column), far apart, or stack them as one compact block composed by balance. What fails is the in-between.

**Few axes, shared edges.** One or two alignment axes per piece (a centre axis, a left margin column, a second column), and every element sits on one of them, the logo included; never centred and left mixed without a reason. Elements side by side share their horizontal edges too: a text block beside an image starts on its top edge or ends on its bottom, a rule meets the edge of the photos it runs along, an image beside the signature ends on the mark's edge (*"bring the text up to the image's top line"* (*"sube el texto de ten days a la linea top de la imagen"*)). After you move or widen one, check the other direction again.

**One margin.** About 8–9 % of the width (88–96 on 1080) on every side, as in the pieces he signed; a side where the format's safe area is deeper (§10) takes the safe area. Images bleed to the canvas edge or stop exactly on the margin, never in between.

**A repertoire, not a formula.** Full-bleed photo with a dark scrim; a split near 62/38; a typographic funnel; a hero number; an oversized quotation; a ruled list or table; a diptych or triptych of equal images; an arch or window mask; a fading or repeated word; a masthead. The content of each piece chooses its composition: explore the repertoire per piece, never sort a catalogue into types. Pieces in one catalogue may have different axes and sign in different places.

**The funnel.** A centred stack can read from big to small: *"a kind of visual funnel… it reads from bigger to smaller"* (*"se genera como un embudo visual… se lee de más grande a más pequeño"*). In his test the display title was narrower than the image above it (about 85 % of its width) and wider than every line below it. When a big title and a big image are fused by width, the title takes the image's exact width and the funnel runs from the title down.

**The call to action.** Decide what the reader must understand before acting: on an invitation the action follows the event; on an instruction to act now it may lead. Either way it is unmistakable at reading size.

**Life, and what "bland" means.** A tidy composition can still feel *"insipid"* (*"insípidas"*), *"bland"* (*"soso"*), *"boring"* (*"aburrido"*) or *"flat"* (*"plano"*). Bring life inside the piece's own language: a bigger, bolder protagonist; a stronger crop; real scale contrast; an asymmetric mass with its counterweight; type on a calm area of the image; a meaningful device from the piece's own vocabulary given more presence. Never answer blandness with ornaments, icons or a new style: *"you can put colour and little more, and that can work perfectly well. The thing is how you place it"* (*"tú puedes poner color y poco más, que eso puede funcionar perfectamente. La cosa es cómo lo pones"*).

**It must make sense.** Each element is where it is for a reason the reader feels: the image beside the words it illustrates, the date beside the event, the rule between the two things it separates. A grid of interchangeable boxes, a shape with no meaning or elements of different families thrown together reads as *"a soup of things"* (*"una sopa de cosas"*) or *"disparate elements"* (*"elementos dispares"*). If you can't say in one sentence why the layout is this way, it isn't designed yet.

**A familiar shape reads before it is explained.** When the piece leans on a symbol (quotation marks, a cross, a book, an arch), look at the render at phone size and name what its silhouette says at first glance. Stretching, cropping or filling it with a photo can change that: photo-filled quotation marks looked to him like angry eyes. Blocks meant as peers stay peers: a step that runs past its neighbour makes *"one block laid over another"* (*"un bloque superpuesto encima del otro"*).

**Space freed is space used.** In a tall piece, what happens on top, the image breathing in the middle and how to attend at the foot is a composition, not a dead gap. A field that feels abandoned is a dead gap: give its room to the protagonist or to the gaps between groups.

## 3. Space

**Distances are visible distances.** Measure between the bodies of the lines (cap height or x-height to the baseline), the visible edges of images, rules and shapes, and the ink of the logo, never between text boxes or from one stray descender. Two gaps equal in the file can look unequal; the eye wins.

**Compose with his scale on his products** (SKILL.md): 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 32, 40, 48, 56, 64, 72, 80, 96, then +16; on someone else's piece, with the base it already repeats. Choose each distance while composing. A measured ink gap off the scale is no fault when the relationship reads right (the gaps in his signed pieces rarely land on it), and gaps are never snapped afterwards: a pass that only snapped gaps left the pieces as they were, and he answered *"the skill barely did anything to the original designs"* (*"el skill apenas hizo nada en los diseños originales"*).

**Equal or clearly different.** Two relationships of the same kind take the same distance, equal within about 2 px on the ink. Two relationships of different kinds differ by a step the eye reads as meant at phone size. Between groups the step is large (Proximity, below). Inside one compact stack it can be small: in his test he set 48 where the photo met the title against 40 from the title to its lede, *"a little more air… another kind of content begins"* (*"un poquito más de aire… empieza otro tipo de contenido"*). A near miss between two gaps that should match (44 and 48) reads as a mistake.

**Between two neighbours: optically centred.** His most repeated spatial note. An element that sits between two others, vertically or horizontally, has the same clear space on both sides, ink to ink: a title between a photo and the next line, a word between two rules, a number between the text above and below, an illustration between the top edge and the title, a row's text between its rules. *"The title doesn't have the same distance between the image and 'Friday'"* (*"el titulo no tiene misma distancia entre la imagen y friday"*). Only when the element clearly belongs to one neighbour does it sit closer to it, and then clearly closer.

**Proximity: close inside, far between.**
- A title and the line that completes or develops it stay close: in the posts he signed, the gap at a change of role ran about 0.7–1.6× the smaller text's size (median about 1×; in his test, 40 between a display title and a 40 px lede), against about 0.3–0.4× where the two roles fused. Never as far as the next group: *"too much distance between title and description"* (*"demasiada distancia entre titulo y descripcion"*); *"title and the text below too close"* (*"titulo y texto de debajo muy junto"*). He asks for both, on different pieces; the rule is the relationship.
- Gaps between groups are clearly larger than gaps inside a group: about twice or more in the pieces he signed.
- An author goes with its quotation, a reference with its verse, the hour with the event it times.

**Text never nearly touches.** Text keeps at least the gap between groups from an image edge, a frame, a band's edge and a rule; his tested rule had 40 on each side, and nothing goes under 24. *"The texts almost touch the borders of the shape"* (*"los textos llegan casi a tocar los border de la figura"*). The exception is type deliberately set on the image as one lockup.

**Rules get equal air.** A rule between two groups is centred optically between their ink (his test: 40 · 2 px rule · 40, nudged to 42/38 when a descender-heavy line sits above). A list stays farther from its framing rule than its rows are from each other.

**Compression and its opposite.** *"Everything compressed, as if forced in"* (*"todo comprimido como metido forzosamente"*) is his most common rejection of a foot or a story; *"everything super expanded"* (*"todo super expandido"*) is the rarer opposite. Do a vertical sweep: every change of role has a visible pause, and no gap is a dead band.

**Yield order when space is short.** First the empty field and the free space outside a box; then the image (shorter, re-cropped: *"if there's no room, then you have to reduce the image's height in that format"* (*"si no hay espacio entonces hay que reducir altura de la imagen en ese formato"*)); then a slightly smaller hero that still clearly leads (on one piece he asked for the big letters *"a bit smaller"* (*"un poco mas pequeñas"*)); last, a modest step down for a long list whose rows can't breathe (*"the list a touch smaller so there's more air between the rows"* (*"el listado un pelin mas pequeño para que haya mas aire entre las filas"*)), never below 24. Never the logo, and never the gaps between groups. When the top has room to spare, move the content up rather than squeezing the foot.

## 4. Type

**Families and styles.**
- When you choose faces, two families at most, contrasting in class (a serif with a grotesk, a script with plain capitals): *"you use two at most"* (*"como mucho se usan dos"*). The face that carries an original's voice keeps its role on top of that.
- One unit, one style: a title, a quotation or a reference is one typeface, one size, one weight. Never a font combination inside one title; the parts of one reference share one size. When the voice face is half of a split title, the split goes, not the voice: set the title in the one face that best carries its tone and give the voice face a role of its own, such as a kicker on its own line.
- Every style owns a role and says something different; a sprawl is one role set two ways or a style that means nothing: *"so many different text styles in so little space… very flashy, very, very inelegant"* (*"tantos estilos de textos diferentes en tan poco espacio… muy cantoso, muy muy poco elegante"*). The count finds a sprawl and is never a target: the pieces he signed carry three to five styles, and in his blind picks the tidier versions with fewer styles won only 10 of 27. A data block is one size; the key datum leads by weight only.
- The face that carries the voice stays, used once and well; in his blind picks the version that kept a voice-carrying third face won 7 of 9. When the face itself is ugly or fights the tone, replace it with a better face of the same class (another script, another condensed). The voice is the face; an outline, a glow or a shadow on it is execution and goes.

**Sizes on 1080.** A short ladder with clear jumps between levels (about 1.4× is a sound default). Two neighbouring levels never read almost alike; where sizes stay close, weight, a column or a block gap tells them apart (48 against 44 in separate blocks in his test). Starting points, widened to his signed pieces: headline 72–132, supporting line 38–56, body 30–44, meta 24–28 in spaced capitals; 24 is his tested floor for small text. A hero word has no ceiling but the width it spans. Size by relation, not only by ratio: set an element against the shapes around it (a date that reaches up beside a photo's silhouette and joins the title's block). In his signed pieces the headline ran 2.5–4× the body. Shrink the protagonist only in the yield order (§3), never below its lead.

**Leading by role.** Display about 1.0–1.1 in his examples (0.85–0.95 on very large type, with accents and descenders clear); headings about 1.05–1.15; body about 1.3–1.45. A paragraph set with heading leading is wrong: *"it isn't a heading, it's a body"* (*"no es una heading es un body"*). A multi-line title is read as one phrase: compare its line gap with its gaps to the image and the text below.

**Tracking by role.** Display slightly negative (about −1 % to −3 %, more on a wide face that spreads its letters); body text at 0, never negative: *"in body texts don't use negative letter spacing"* (*"en textos de body no usar letter spacing negativo"*); spaced capitals at 0 or slightly positive.

**Line breaks.**
- Break by meaning. A title often reads best in two balanced lines; when it helps, set it so.
- No widows, no lone word on the last line, no middle dot or comma ending a line, never a name, a date or a time split ("8:00 / AM").
- Columns in one row get the same number of lines; when one wraps, change the structure (column width, size, wording order) so they align: *"below the line it isn't well aligned with two-line phrases"* (*"debajo de la linea no esta bien alineado con frases de dos lineas"*).
- Two columns meant to start level start level: compare the visible tops of their first letters.

**Words that span a width reach it.** A display word set to the width of an image, a band or the margin reaches both edges: *"I don't like that the word doesn't reach the end"* (*"no me gusta que la palabra… se quede sin llegar al final"*). Adjust its size or tracking, then check the gaps above and below it again.

**Alignment.** Consistent alignment on the piece's axes; a centred block's lines look centred by their ink, not by their boxes.

## 5. Images and gradients

**The composition leads; the image adapts to it.** Decide the structure, the frames and the type for the beauty and hierarchy of the whole, then fit the photograph to its frame: *"the composition, the beauty of the design, comes first"* (*"prima la composición, la belleza del diseño"*). Never shrink, inset, reshape or move a frame, or rebuild a layout, to show a detail of the photo (hands, a bracelet, an accessory): *"it shouldn't look at the image and the elements it contains and adapt the contents to them; it's the image that adapts"* (*"no se debe fijar en la imagen y los elementos que contiene y en base a eso adaptar los contenidos, es la imagen la que se adapta"*). In a template the photo is sample content: the user replaces it and nudges its framing in the editor.

**Crop inside the frame.** Scale the photo to fill its frame and move it until the main subject (a face, the person, the object the piece is about) reads whole and clear of the type; re-crop for each format. Never cut a face, never leave a head touching the edge, never stretch or squash: crop and scale. Losing a secondary detail to the crop is fine.

**Bleed or align, never in between.** An image runs to the canvas edge or stops exactly on the margin or a keyline, stretched to the safe area's sides when the structure calls for it. A photo that bleeds keeps bleeding through a redesign; one cut back so that bars of ground show beside it fails: *"photo cut at the sides instead of expanding to cover the side gaps"* (*"foto cortada en los lados en vez de expandirse y cubrir los huecos laterales"*). Images that sit together as peers (a diptych, a triptych, a grid) share one size and their gutters are equal: *"the images have different widths"* (*"las imagenes tienen anchos distintos"*). Images that sit inside the canvas as objects, or form a structure among themselves, stay inside the safe area: *"the images must respect the safe zone because they're square and form a structure between them"* (*"las imágenes sí deben respetar el safe zone porque son cuadradas y forman una estructura entre sí"*).

**The image is the protagonist when it carries the emotion.** Reducing it to a thumbnail drains the piece. A mask (circle, arch, window) must mean something; compare it with the photo shown directly. A circle photo with a border looks dated; a border that adds nothing goes (*"remove the image's border"* (*"quitar el border de la imagen"*)). An enlarged shape keeps the subject whole: *"the circle much bigger, with only part of it showing at the top"* (*"que el circulo sea mucho mas grande y se vea solo una parte arriba"*) is a valid gesture when the subject survives the crop.

**Scrims are dark.** Behind text on a photo, a long, eased gradient from clear to near-black or a deep tone of the palette, only where the text sits. A mid-grey wash looks dirty: *"not that grey, which looks stale"* (*"no ese gris que es rancio"*).

**Every gradient is smooth.** His absolute rule: *"gradients must always be smooth, so the cut doesn't show"* (*"los gradientes siempre deben ser smooth y que no se note el corte"*).
- Give the fade enough length, ease its stops, and end it on the exact colour of the surface it joins.
- Add 1–2 % noise or dither when a long, dark fade risks banding.
- Inspect the exported image at 100 % along the whole transition, where the photo is brightest and darkest: no line, no band, no step in pace or hue, no detail that vanishes halfway.
- A fade that carries the piece never becomes a solid block; a solid block that interrupts a photograph is a regression he rejects.

## 6. Colour

- **A pair, with roles.** The ground and its accent carry the identity; judge them together. Two hues plus neutrals at most when you choose; the accent goes where the eye must go first, on one thing.
- **A palette that works stays** through a recomposition. Change its proportions before its colours.
- **Every colour belongs to the family.** An off-family tint (a dull lilac band, a muddy brown or olive field, a neon ground) reads as ugly: *"that ugly lilac"* (*"ese lila feo"*), *"ugly colours"* (*"colores feos"*). A new dark field is near-black or a clean deep tone of the palette.
- **Text on colour or photo:** when size, weight or face already separate the levels, one flat colour at full strength. When levels sit close, the headline and key datum at 100 %, secondary text at about 60–64 %, as few tones as possible.
- **A colour code needs a meaning the reader can see**, or it becomes noise.

## 7. Devices, lines and icons

Cover each device, then put it back: does it carry information, the concept or the structure? Keep what does and execute it cleanly; remove what doesn't.

| Goes | Stays |
|---|---|
| An icon too small to read: *"it looks tiny and ridiculous"* (*"se ve enano y ridiculo"*) | A table, a bar chart or a schedule that lets the reader compare: *"you removed the little table"* (*"eliminaste la tablita"*) |
| An icon that repeats a symbol the logo already has | The original's fade, band or strip when it is part of its identity |
| A seal or stamp that looks fake | A rule that forms an axis, anchors a quotation or evens a rhythm |
| Decorative squares, boxes or bullets (*"unnecessary little squares"* (*"cuadraditos innecesarios"*)) | A frame that gives an image its shape |
| A border around a photo; underlines under data (*"they create noise"* (*"generan ruido"*)) | A repetition that gives the piece its rhythm |
| Lines that separate nothing, or too many of them (*"too many lines"* (*"demasidas lineas"*)) | A meaningful motif that expresses the subject |

- An icon stays only when it reads at phone size (about the cap height of its text or larger), comes from one family at one opacity, and carries information.
- Hairlines are 2–3 px at 1080 so they survive the phone.
- **Concentric curves.** A line or a frame inside a curved block keeps a constant strip through the curve: inner radius = outer radius − inset on a convex corner, + inset around a concave cut-out; arcs meant to run parallel share one centre. Check the strip on the straight, at the join and halfway through the turn: *"the line's roundness isn't done right to be consistent with the negative border radius of the corners"* (*"no se hace bien la redondez de la linea para ser consistente con el border radius negativo de loas esquinas"*).

## 8. Lists, tables and practical data

- **Rows:** each row's content is centred between its rules, baselines align across columns, and every row reads as one unit. The list reaches the full width it implies.
- **Rhythm:** rows breathe for their type size, neither cramped nor drifting apart (*"less air between the rows"* (*"menos aire entre las filas"*) on one piece, *"so the checklist breathes"* (*"que el checklist respire"*) on another: the relationship decides). In his test, 40 px data rows read comfortably 20 apart: *"a touch of air… very easy to read"* (*"un pelín de aire… muy fácil de leer"*). The gap between rows is smaller than the gap between the list and its title or its framing rule.
- **Practical data** (when, where, how much, how to book) is its own level, categorised by line breaks, order and gaps, with the key datum first and leading by weight, and its figures big. Never one uniform small block, and never new labels or styles to categorise it. Inline items in one category take a middle dot (day · time). Keep the hour beside the event it times.
- **Don't pile everything at the top.** A kicker, the title, a description and an availability line stacked together *"overload the top"* (*"sobrecarga arriba"*): spread them across the groups.
- **Icons in stacked rows** only where they help scanning: one set, one size and one opacity for the whole set (about 64 %), never one tone per row: *"that way you don't create visual noise by putting in a lot of shades of white"* (*"así no se genera ruido visual poniendo un montón de tonalidades de blanco"*).
- **A post is not a flyer.** On a one-off post, keep what the reader needs to act; move the rest to the caption and list what moved. A template keeps its slots.

## 9. The signature

The logo slot is part of the composition, designed in, never counted as free space. On a piece a customer brands, the author of the template decides where the slot goes; a redesign can move it.

- **Where.** At the end of the reading: the foot, in a corner on one of the content's axes, or centred on a row of its own below the last group (*"on a new row at the very bottom"* (*"en una fila nueva abajo del todo"*), *"under the phrase"* (*"debajo de la frase"*)); or at the top, as the sender or a masthead, when the top is the end the content leaves free: the foot holds the call to action or the practical data, or leaves no calm corner. Inside the safe area, reaching its edge or corner (*"right to the very edge, up to the safe zone"* (*"derecha del todo hasta zona de seguridad"*)), on a calm ground. A corner works only where the content uses that edge as an axis: a centred piece signs centred, at the foot or at the top. In a corner, it takes the side the content leaves free, opposite the last line it balances.
- **Clear of the text.** Find the line of text that comes nearest the mark, above, beside or diagonally: it keeps at least the gap between groups, so the reader finishes that line before the eye jumps to the signature. Never floating on a busy part of a photo, sharing a line with the title or the call to action as their peer, or *"loose, for no reason"* (*"suelto sin snetido"*).
- **Aligned with what it faces.** By default its visible bottom sits on the baseline of the last line it faces (*"the logo isn't aligned at the very bottom with the last phrase"* (*"el logo no esta alineado abajo del todo con la ultima frase"*)); or its top on the cap height of the first line when that relationship leads. Its height is about the height of the two lines it faces: *"to create visual harmony and avoid visual jumps"* (*"para crear armonia visual y evitar saltos visuales"*). Measure the printed artwork inside the logo file, including its transparent padding.
- **Size.** It signs, it never competes with the headline, and it is never a speck. In the pieces he signed the signature stood about 80–90 px tall; a wordmark's letters are no smaller than the piece's smallest text. Fit the mark by its shape: a wide wordmark takes the slot's width, a square or round emblem its height. Keep the same px in every format: *"in story format the logos come out tiny"* (*"en formato historia los logos salen enanos"*). If there is no room, the image yields (§3).
- **Ground and version.** The mark meant for its ground; if a bright mark jumps ahead of the information it signs, quiet it (a little opacity where the brand allows) or move it.
- **Optional branding.** Hide the mark once: the composition still feels complete where the signature was.

## 10. Formats

- **Each format is its own composition**, with the same hierarchy and the same DNA. The logo keeps the same px in every format (all share the 1080 width) and no text gets smaller in a taller format; the protagonist may grow where a format gives it room. Never derive one format by stretching or scaling another.
- **The story's extra height** goes to the protagonist, the image or the pauses between groups, decided on the story's own render; never to stretched gaps inside a group. Check the story on its own at phone size: it is where pieces most often come out compressed and the signature tiny.
- **The square is the tightest:** solve its foot first, then compose each taller format on its own.
- **Safe areas are a hard limit.** Every text, the logo and key content (a face, a date, a code) stay inside; a photo, colour field or texture that bleeds on purpose may cross. Trace the whole contour of a framed object, not only its text. The product's own safe area wins; otherwise the platform's (px):

  | Format | Top | Sides | Bottom |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Feed square 1080×1080 | 80 | 80 | 80 |
  | Feed portrait 1080×1350 (4:5) | 96 | 80 | 120 |
  | Story or reel 1080×1920 (Instagram, Facebook) | 250 | 64 left, 100 right | 420 |
  | TikTok 1080×1920 | 170 | 64 left, 120 right | 280 |
  | YouTube Shorts 1080×1920 | 260 | 90 | 260 |

  A 3:4 portrait (1080×1440) is common too: render at the canvas the product exports and use its safe area; this table never sets the canvas. A protected strip still belongs to the composition: a background can run through it, and the content above it still needs an anchor.

## 11. Templates other people fill

- **Real content.** Every editable slot survives a shorter and a longer text, a light and a dark photo, a wordmark and a round emblem. Swap one text per format and look again.
- **Reusable.** A template will carry other words for the same occasion, by people or by an automated assistant. A gesture so tied to one sentence that it works for nothing else fails: *"the design is beautiful but it will only serve for that prayer"* (*"el diseño es bonito pero no servira mas que para esa oracion"*).
- **Photos it doesn't own.** A detail of the sample photo never decides the layout. When the user replaces the photo, its calm area is unknown: text and the logo over a photo sit on a dark scrim or a fixed field, or off the photo.
- **Native, editable layers.** Keep every editable role and asset; work in the template's own layers, not on a flattened image.
- **Emails:** one column, one main action, readable sizes on mobile, and images that are not essential to understand the message.
- **A catalogue is not a series.** Distinct templates may differ in axes and signature position; a coordinated series shares its grid, margins, type and signature. Don't add a template whose composition another already has.

## 12. The gates

Run every gate on the final render, at phone size and at 100 %, in every format. Any failure sends the piece back. A design is presented only when every gate passes and the verdict is *I'd sign it*; a requested retouch is shown when the change is right and gate 15 passes, with the other faults reported. Gates 1–3 are answered only by looking; a script can locate faults for the others and never passes any.

1. **Better and the same family.** Side by side with the original, every defect named in the diagnosis is gone, nothing that worked is lost, and it is still recognisably the same template; a recomposition can't be mistaken for the original at feed size, and it beats a polish of the original's own composition. Or it is the original unchanged because it already signs. A drift fails.
2. **The protagonist** is at least as strong as in the original; no face or main subject is cut; a familiar shape still reads as itself at phone size.
3. **The reading order** 1 → 2 → 3 is obvious at thumbnail size; nothing competes for first place.
4. **Type:** two families at most when you choose them, plus the original's voice face where it has a role; one face per title; every style with a role and no role set two ways; one size per data block; no two neighbouring levels that read almost alike; smallest text 24 or more; body tracking 0; leading by role.
5. **Space:** equal relationships equal, different ones clearly different; inside a group closer than between groups; no compression, no dead band. The distances you set came from his scale (or the piece's own base) while composing, and none was snapped afterwards.
6. **Centring:** every element between two neighbours has the same clear space on both sides, ink to ink, unless it clearly belongs to one.
7. **Rows, columns and lists:** content centred between rules, baselines aligned, equal line counts, full intended width.
8. **Line breaks:** by meaning; no widow, no line-ending dot, no split time, name or date.
9. **Edges:** one margin; everything inside the safe area; images bleed or align; peers equal; shared edges meet; display words reach the width they imply; nothing nearly touches.
10. **Images and gradients:** every image fills its frame and adapts to the composition, never the reverse; every fade checked at 100 % with no seam or band.
11. **Devices:** each earns its place; no tiny icon, fake seal, decorative box or surplus line; curves concentric.
12. **The signature:** on an axis or in a safe corner, clear of the nearest line, aligned with what it faces, legible at phone size, the same px in every format, never floating; the piece complete without it.
13. **Formats:** each viewed on its own at phone size; the story not compressed, its signature not tiny, no text smaller than in the square.
14. **Robustness:** a shorter and a longer text still work; the template serves other words for its occasion.
15. **Scope:** on a requested retouch, only that changed, proved against the previous render.

## 13. Measuring a render

- Measure ink and visible edges, never boxes. When the product's render tool reports the ink box of each element, its margins and the gaps between stacked elements, read those figures instead of zooming for each distance. Otherwise, or to confirm what figures can't tell (an optical edge, an overhang, a hairline), zoom a crop to 100 % and read the pixel rows where letters, rules, images and the mark begin and end.
- When the renderer can give you the rectangles of visible ink, `scripts/measure-ink-field.py` compares the clear space on both sides of an element in the field you name.
- For a piece built in HTML, `scripts/measure-graphic.py file.html --size 1080x1350` (use the real canvas; add `--safe top,right,bottom,left` for the product's safe area) reports margins, gaps between letter bodies, styles, axes, the safe area and the logo. Inspect each FAIL or WARN on the render.
- Measurement locates a fault and confirms a fix. It never passes a piece: a clean report means only that its checks passed. The verdict is the eye's, on the render, beside the original.

## 14. Logos and identity

- Derive the concept from the content. In one media identity he designed, the rising sun in the mark takes its meaning from the accompanying Benedictus quotation.
- The mark holds at small sizes and in one colour.
- The mark and the wordmark are optically balanced, with consistent strokes and corner treatment.
- The lockup and its clear space are defined.
- A short palette with roles; type that matches the tone.
